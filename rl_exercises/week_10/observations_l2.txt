LEVEL 2: MULTI-FIDELITY IN RL -- WHEN ARE EARLY SCORES DECEIVING?

To run the experiment, we execute
`python rl_exercises/week_10/l2_multifidelity.py` (inside the .venv).

Designed setting and why early scores can deceive
-------------------------------------------------
We tune tabular Q-Learning (alpha, epsilon, gamma) on the stochastic MarsRover,
with a search space that allows very large learning rates (alpha up to 1.0).
A large alpha bootstraps the Q-values towards a good policy extremely fast, so
after only a few hundred steps such a configuration often already has a great
evaluation return. But a large learning rate keeps overreacting to the
environment stochasticity, so with more training the Q-values oscillate and the
return collapses. Fidelity = number of training steps -> the low-fidelity score
(500 steps) is thus a poor predictor of the high-fidelity score (8000 steps).

Part A: low- vs high-fidelity correlation
-----------------------------------------
Source data: l2_fidelity_scores.csv, Visualization: l2_low_vs_high.pdf
(60 random configs, scores averaged over seeds 0/1/2.)

    Spearman(low-fidelity 500 steps, high-fidelity 8000 steps) = 0.454

The correlation is weak, i.e. the early ranking only loosely matches the final
ranking. Concretely:

| Selected by            | Config | Score @500 | Final @8000 |
|------------------------|--------|------------|-------------|
| best low-fidelity      | #21    | 61.39      | 46.49       |
| best high-fidelity     | #7     | 57.68      | 61.81       |

Config #21 is the most attractive after 500 steps but loses a quarter of its
performance by 8000 steps; the truly best config #7 is only mid-table early.
An aggressive "keep whatever looks best early" strategy would pick #21 and end
up 15.32 return (25%) below the real optimum. Five of the top-5 low-fidelity
configs drop out of the top-5 at high fidelity ("deceptive"), and five of the
top-5 high-fidelity configs would have been pruned early ("overlooked").

Part B: does a real multi-fidelity optimizer get misled?
--------------------------------------------------------
We ran Optuna's SuccessiveHalvingPruner (TPE sampler, 40 trials, fidelities
500/1000/2000/4000/8000 steps) and inspected its optimization history.

| Metric                                   | Value  |
|------------------------------------------|--------|
| Completed trials                         | 14     |
| Pruned trials                            | 26     |
| Best full-fidelity value found           | 62.46  |
| Pruned trials that were actually good    | 0      |

Observation: SuccessiveHalving did NOT get trapped. Although early scores are
deceiving (Part A), the pruner still keeps several configurations all the way
to full budget, and the strong low-alpha configurations happen to be good
enough early to survive the first rungs. So the pruned study still recovered a
near-optimal configuration (62.46), and no pruned trial would have beaten it.

Conclusion
----------
Good initial performance CAN influence the optimization negatively, but the
severity depends on how aggressively partial evaluations are trusted:

    - Naive selection purely by the low-fidelity score is badly misled here:
      it costs 25% of the final return, because the fastest early learners are
      the unstable high-alpha configurations.
    - A properly designed multi-fidelity optimizer (successive halving that
      still promotes several configs to full budget) is far more robust and
      recovered a top configuration despite the weak early-vs-final
      correlation.

The pitfall is therefore real, and the practical lesson is to not prune too
aggressively when the low-fidelity signal is weakly correlated with the final
objective -- exactly the situation created by high learning rates in a
stochastic environment.
